Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning · Chapter 16 · §16.3

Put a small bump on every data point. Add them up. That's a density.

Kernel density estimation (KDE) builds a probability distribution \(p(\mathbf x)\) straight from the data, without choosing a family like "Gaussian" first. Each training example contributes one small, symmetric bump called a kernel. The estimate is the average of the bumps. Like KNN in §16.1, it keeps all the training data, so it is nonparametric.

KDE is a generative model: you can evaluate \(p(\mathbf x)\) at any point, and you can sample new data from it. The same idea also gives a classifier (KNN, derived properly this time) and a regression method (Nadaraya-Watson kernel regression and its local-linear cousin, LOWESS).

§16.3.1

Density kernels: a small, symmetric bump with area 1

"Kernel" means several different things in machine learning. In this section a density kernel is a function \(K:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R_+\) with two properties: it integrates to 1, \(\int K(x)\,dx=1\), and it is symmetric, \(K(-x)=K(x)\). So a density kernel is a probability density centered at 0.

Symmetry makes its mean 0, so a kernel shifted to sit on a data point \(x_n\) has mean exactly \(x_n\) (Eq. 16.25). A bandwidth \(h\) stretches the bump sideways and squashes it down so the area stays 1 (Eq. 16.28).

Explain itAt your level

Think of a scoop of sand.

You pour one scoop on the floor and it makes a little hill. The hill is tallest in the middle and the same on both sides. No matter how you spread it, you always have exactly one scoop of sand.

If you spread it wide, the hill gets flat and low. If you keep it narrow, the hill gets tall and pointy. The amount of sand never changes. How wide you spread it is called the bandwidth.

Different kernels are different hill shapes: a flat box, a round dome, or a smooth bell.

A kernel is a curve with three rules: it's never negative, the area under it is exactly 1, and its left half is a mirror image of its right half.

The simplest is the boxcar: a rectangle of height 0.5 from −1 to 1. Width 2 times height 0.5 gives area 1. The Gaussian is the bell curve.

The bandwidth \(h\) stretches the curve. If you make it twice as wide, you have to make it half as tall to keep the area at 1. That's why the formula is \(\frac1h K(\frac xh)\): dividing \(x\) by \(h\) stretches it, and the \(\frac1h\) out front squashes it.

Because it's symmetric, the balance point (the mean) of a kernel centered at \(x_n\) is \(x_n\) itself.

A density kernel is a zero-mean, symmetric pdf: \(K\ge0\), \(\int K=1\), \(K(-x)=K(x)\). Symmetry makes \(xK(x)\) an odd function, so \(\int xK(x)\,dx=0\). Substituting \(u=x-x_n\) gives Eq. 16.25: \(\int xK(x-x_n)\,dx=x_n\).

The scaled kernel \(K_h(x)=\frac1hK(\frac xh)\) is still a pdf (substitute \(u=x/h\)), and its standard deviation is \(h\) times that of \(K\).

Table 16.1 compares kernels on three properties. Compact support (zero outside \([-1,1]\)) means far-away points contribute nothing, which is faster to compute. Smooth means differentiable inside the support. Boundaries means also differentiable at \(\pm1\), where the kernel meets zero. Only the tri-cube has all three; the Gaussian is smooth but never exactly zero.

In \(D\) dimensions, an RBF kernel depends only on \(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\). The Gaussian RBF factors into a product of 1-D Gaussians (Eq. 16.30).

  • Not a Mercer kernel. A density kernel is a pdf on the input space, used for smoothing. A Mercer (positive definite) kernel \(\mathcal K(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')\) is an inner product in a feature space, used in GPs and SVMs (Ch. 17). The Gaussian happens to be both, which is why the names blur.
  • Kernel order. A second-order kernel has \(\int K=1\), \(\int xK=0\), \(\mu_2(K)=\int x^2K>0\). Every kernel here is second order. Higher-order kernels set \(\mu_2=0\) to cancel the \(h^2\) bias term, at the price of taking negative values.
  • Efficiency. Under the asymptotic MISE criterion, the Epanechnikov kernel is optimal; the Gaussian is about 95% as efficient, tri-cube and boxcar about 99% and 93%. The choice of kernel matters far less than the choice of \(h\).
  • Compact support in practice. With compact support, KDE evaluation only touches points within \(h\) of the query, so a k-d tree or grid gives near \(O(\log N)\) queries. With a Gaussian, people truncate at a few \(h\) or use fast Gauss transforms / FFT binning.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.25 – 16.30

Why the shifted kernel has mean \(x_n\)

\[ \int x\,K(x-x_n)\,dx = x_n \tag{16.25} \]
  1. Substitute \(u=x-x_n\), so \(x=u+x_n\) and \(dx=du\). This slides the kernel back to center 0. \[ \int (u+x_n)\,K(u)\,du=\int uK(u)\,du + x_n\int K(u)\,du \]
  2. \(uK(u)\) is odd: \((-u)K(-u)=-uK(u)\) because \(K(-u)=K(u)\). An odd function integrates to 0 over a symmetric range, so the first term vanishes.
  3. \(\int K(u)\,du=1\). That's the normalization rule. What's left is \(0+x_n\cdot1=x_n\).

This is the fact that makes Nadaraya-Watson regression work in §16.3.5.1.

Two kernels, checked by hand

\[ K_{\text{box}}(x)=\tfrac12\,\mathbb I(|x|\le1) \tag{16.26} \qquad\int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac12\,dx=\tfrac12\cdot2=1 \]
\[ K_{\text{Epa}}(x)=\tfrac34(1-x^2)\,\mathbb I(|x|\le1),\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}\tfrac34(1-x^2)\,dx=\tfrac34\Big[x-\tfrac{x^3}3\Big]_{-1}^{1}=\tfrac34\Big(2-\tfrac23\Big)=1 \]
\[ K_{\text{Gauss}}(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}e^{-x^2/2} \tag{16.27} \]

The \(\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}\) is there because \(\int e^{-x^2/2}dx=\sqrt{2\pi}\).

The bandwidth keeps the area at 1

\[ K_h(x)\triangleq\frac1h\,K\!\Big(\frac xh\Big) \tag{16.28} \]
  1. Substitute \(u=x/h\), so \(dx=h\,du\): \(\int\frac1hK(\frac xh)\,dx=\int\frac1hK(u)\,h\,du=\int K(u)\,du=1\). The \(\frac1h\) exactly cancels the stretch.
  2. The boxcar with bandwidth \(h\) is \(\frac1{2h}\) on \([-h,h]\). Width \(2h\), height \(\frac1{2h}\), area 1.
  3. Variance: \(\int x^2K_h(x)\,dx=h^2\int u^2K(u)\,du\). So the standard deviation scales with \(h\). A Gaussian kernel with bandwidth \(h\) is just \(\mathcal N(0,h^2)\).

Vector inputs

\[ K_h(\mathbf x)\propto K_h(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert) \tag{16.29} \]
\[ K_h(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{h^D(2\pi)^{D/2}}\prod_{d=1}^{D}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{1}{2h^2}x_d^2\Big) \tag{16.30} \]

A product of exponentials is the exponential of a sum: \(\prod_d e^{-x_d^2/2h^2}=e^{-\lVert\mathbf x\rVert^2/2h^2}\). So the product form depends only on \(\lVert\mathbf x\rVert\): it is radial. Each axis contributes a factor \(\frac1{h\sqrt{2\pi}}\), which gives the constant \(\frac1{h^D(2\pi)^{D/2}}\). This is the density of \(\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,h^2I)\).

Why use compact kernels at all? With the Gaussian, every data point contributes something to every query, so evaluating a KDE costs \(O(N)\) per query no matter what. With compact support, points farther than \(h\) contribute exactly 0 and can be skipped with a spatial index.

§16.3.2

The Parzen window estimator: one bump per data point

Start from a Gaussian mixture model with \(K\) clusters, all with the same spherical covariance and equal weights (Eq. 16.31). Picking \(K\) and the centers \(\boldsymbol\mu_k\) is the hard part. KDE skips it: put one cluster on every data point (Eq. 16.32), then allow any density kernel (Eq. 16.33).

The result is the Parzen window density estimator, also called the kernel density estimator. Nothing is fitted except the bandwidth \(h\). The price: you store all \(N\) points, and every evaluation touches all of them.

Explain itAt your level

Drop one scoop of sand everywhere you found a seashell.

Where lots of shells were close together, the sand piles up into a big hill. Where there was just one shell, you get a little bump. Where there were none, the floor stays flat.

Now the height of the sand tells you where shells are likely to be found. That's the whole idea. If you want to guess where the next shell might be, pick a random scoop and look somewhere inside it.

Flat, wide scoops make one big smooth hill. Narrow, pointy scoops make a spike at every shell.

You have \(N\) data points on a number line. Draw a kernel bump centered on each one, make each bump \(\frac1N\) as tall (so each has area \(\frac1N\)), and add them all up. The total area is \(N\cdot\frac1N=1\), so the sum is a probability density.

With the boxcar kernel, this is just counting. At any spot \(x\), count the data points within \(h\) of \(x\), divide by \(N\) and by the window width \(2h\). It looks like a histogram, but the "bins" slide along with \(x\) instead of being fixed in place.

With the Gaussian kernel, each point's vote fades smoothly with distance, so the result is a smooth curve.

Eq. 16.33: \(p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^NK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\). It's an equal-weight mixture of \(N\) copies of the kernel, one centered on each training point. Each component is a pdf, so the average is a pdf.

Compared with a GMM: no \(K\) to choose, no EM, no local optima. The cost moves to prediction time: \(O(ND)\) memory and \(O(ND)\) per evaluation.

Because it's a mixture, sampling is easy: pick \(n\) uniformly from \(\{1,\dots,N\}\), then draw \(\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim K\) and return \(\mathbf x_n+h\boldsymbol\epsilon\). That's what "generative" means here.

Figure 16.9 shows the two extremes. The boxcar gives a piecewise-constant estimate with jumps at \(x_n\pm h\). The Gaussian is smooth. Doubling \(h\) flattens both.

  • Consistency. If \(h\to0\) and \(Nh^D\to\infty\), \(\hat p\to p\) in mean square at every continuity point (Parzen, 1962). The first condition kills bias, the second kills variance.
  • Bias and variance. \(\mathbb E\hat p(x)=(K_h*p)(x)=p(x)+\tfrac{h^2}2\mu_2(K)p''(x)+o(h^2)\): KDE is unbiased for the smoothed density, and it flattens peaks and fills valleys. \(\mathrm{Var}\,\hat p(x)\approx\frac{p(x)R(K)}{Nh^D}\), with \(R(K)=\int K^2\).
  • Rates. Balancing gives MISE \(=O(N^{-4/(4+D)})\), slower than the parametric \(O(N^{-1})\) and much slower as \(D\) grows. This is the curse of dimensionality from §16.1.2 again.
  • Practice. scikit-learn's KernelDensity uses a k-d / ball tree with tolerances; SciPy's gaussian_kde uses a full covariance bandwidth \(H=h^2\hat\Sigma\). For 1-D plots, binned FFT KDE (as in seaborn/statsmodels) evaluates on a grid in \(O(G\log G)\).
  • Boundaries. For data on \([0,\infty)\), a symmetric kernel leaks mass below 0. Fixes: reflection, boundary kernels, or a log transform.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.31 – 16.33

From a mixture model to KDE

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\sigma^2I) \tag{16.31} \]
  1. This is a GMM with equal weights \(\frac1K\) and one shared spherical covariance \(\sigma^2I\). You still have to choose \(K\) and learn the centers \(\boldsymbol\mu_k\), for example with EM.
  2. Set \(K=N\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu_n=\mathbf x_n\): one cluster per data point. Nothing left to learn except \(\sigma\). \[ p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_n,\sigma^2I) \tag{16.32} \]
  3. \(\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_n,\sigma^2I)\) is the Gaussian kernel \(K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\) with \(h=\sigma\) (Eq. 16.30). Allow any density kernel: This is the Parzen window / kernel density estimator. \[ p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n) \tag{16.33} \]

It is a valid density

Each \(K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\ge0\), so the sum is \(\ge0\). And \(\int p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)\,d\mathbf x=\frac1N\sum_n\int K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\,d\mathbf x=\frac1N\sum_n1=1\). The "total area" readout in the Play box checks this numerically.

The boxcar version is a sliding count

With \(K_h(u)=\frac1{2h}\mathbb I(|u|\le h)\) in 1-D:

\[ p(x\mid\mathcal D)=\frac1N\sum_{n}\frac{1}{2h}\mathbb I(|x-x_n|\le h)=\frac{\#\{n: |x-x_n|\le h\}}{N\cdot2h} \]

Worked example (Figure 16.9a). Data \(\{-2.1,-1.3,-0.4,1.9,5.1,6.2\}\), \(h=1\), query \(x=-1.2\). The points within 1 of \(-1.2\) are \(-2.1\) (distance 0.9), \(-1.3\) (0.1) and \(-0.4\) (0.8). So

\[ p(-1.2\mid\mathcal D)=\frac{3}{6\cdot2}=0.25 \]

That's the tallest step in Figure 16.9a. Put the query at \(-1.2\) in the Play box with the boxcar and \(h=1\) to check.

Sampling

  1. Draw \(n\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,N\}\). Each mixture component has weight \(\frac1N\).
  2. Draw \(\epsilon\sim K\) (the unscaled kernel) and return \(x=x_n+h\epsilon\). \(h\epsilon\) has density \(K_h\), so \(x\) has density \(K_h(x-x_n)\).
Cost. Memory is \(O(ND)\): the whole dataset is the model. Each evaluation of \(p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)\) sums \(N\) kernels, \(O(ND)\). A GMM with \(K\ll N\) components costs \(O(KD)\) per evaluation after training.

§16.3.3

Choosing the bandwidth: too narrow is spiky, too wide is blurry

The bandwidth \(h\) controls model complexity. Small \(h\): a spike on every point, low bias, high variance (overfitting). Large \(h\): one smooth blob, high bias, low variance (underfitting).

If the data really are Gaussian and you use a Gaussian kernel, the best \(h\) (minimizing frequentist risk) is \(h=\sigma\big(\frac{4}{3N}\big)^{1/5}\). Estimate \(\sigma\) robustly with the median absolute deviation: \(\hat\sigma=1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}\).

Explain itAt your level

How wide should each scoop of sand be?

Too narrow, and you get a sharp little tower at each shell, with empty floor in between. That says "shells can only be exactly where we already found them," which is silly.

Too wide, and everything melts into one big flat pile. You can't tell that shells come in two groups anymore.

The right width is in the middle. And the more shells you've found, the narrower your scoops can be, because the shells themselves already fill in the gaps.

The bandwidth is like the "blur" slider on a photo. No blur: every speck of noise shows. Lots of blur: you lose the real details.

There's a recipe for a good starting value, called a rule of thumb: \(h=\sigma\,(4/(3N))^{1/5}\), where \(\sigma\) is the spread (standard deviation) of the data and \(N\) is how many points you have. For 100 points with \(\sigma=1\), that's about 0.42.

Notice the power \(\frac15\): to cut \(h\) in half, you need \(2^5=32\) times as much data.

The spread can be thrown off by a few wild points. A safer way to measure it: find the middle value (median), measure how far each point is from it, and take the median of those distances. Multiply by 1.4826 and you get \(\sigma\) for normal-looking data.

The rule \(h=\sigma(4/3N)^{1/5}\approx1.06\,\sigma N^{-1/5}\) minimizes the asymptotic mean integrated squared error (AMISE) when both the true density and the kernel are Gaussian ([BA97a]; it's often called Silverman's rule).

Robust scale. \(\mathrm{MAD}=\mathrm{median}(|x-\mathrm{median}(x)|)\). For a Gaussian, \(\mathrm{MAD}=\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\,\sigma\approx0.6745\,\sigma\), so \(\hat\sigma=\mathrm{MAD}/0.6745=1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}\). A few outliers can blow up the sample standard deviation, but they barely move a median.

D dimensions. Compute \(h_d\) for each dimension separately and use the geometric mean \(h=(\prod_dh_d)^{1/D}\).

The rule assumes a Gaussian truth, so it oversmooths multimodal data. Try the "two bumps" dataset in the Play box. A data-driven alternative is to pick \(h\) that maximizes the leave-one-out log-likelihood.

  • AMISE. \(\mathrm{AMISE}(h)=\frac{h^4}4\mu_2(K)^2R(p'')+\frac{R(K)}{Nh}\). Setting the derivative to 0 gives \(h^*=\big[\frac{R(K)}{\mu_2(K)^2R(p'')N}\big]^{1/5}\). With a Gaussian \(K\) (\(R(K)=\frac1{2\sqrt\pi}\), \(\mu_2=1\)) and Gaussian \(p\) (\(R(p'')=\frac{3}{8\sqrt\pi\sigma^5}\)), you get exactly \(\sigma(4/3N)^{1/5}\). The optimal AMISE then decreases like \(N^{-4/5}\).
  • Silverman's robust version uses \(0.9\min(\hat\sigma,\mathrm{IQR}/1.34)N^{-1/5}\) to hedge against multimodality and heavy tails.
  • Data-driven selectors. Least-squares (unbiased) cross-validation minimizes an unbiased estimate of ISE; likelihood CV maximizes \(\sum_i\log\hat p_{-i}(x_i)\) and is sensitive to heavy tails (the outlier term dominates). Plug-in selectors (Sheather–Jones) estimate \(R(p'')\) with a pilot KDE and are the usual default in R.
  • Multivariate. The general form uses a bandwidth matrix, \(K_H(\mathbf x)=|H|^{-1/2}K(H^{-1/2}\mathbf x)\). Scott's rule: \(H^{1/2}=N^{-1/(D+4)}\hat\Sigma^{1/2}\). The book's geometric mean collapses \(H\) to \(h^2I\), so standardize features first.
  • Adaptive bandwidths. Sample-point (Abramson) estimators use \(h_n\propto\hat p(\mathbf x_n)^{-1/2}\): wide where data are sparse. The balloon estimator in §16.3.4 adapts \(h\) to the query instead.

Math, slowlyThe rule of thumb and the MAD

The optimal bandwidth for Gaussian data

\[ h=\sigma\Big(\frac{4}{3N}\Big)^{1/5}\approx1.06\,\sigma\,N^{-1/5} \]
  1. \((4/3)^{1/5}=e^{\frac15\ln(1.333)}=e^{0.0575}\approx1.059\). That's where the familiar 1.06 comes from.
  2. Worked example. \(\sigma=1\), \(N=100\): \(\frac{4}{300}=0.01333\), and \(0.01333^{1/5}=e^{\frac15\ln0.01333}=e^{-0.8635}\approx0.42\). So \(h\approx0.42\).
  3. \(h\) is proportional to \(\sigma\). If you measure the data in centimeters instead of meters, the bandwidth scales with it, as it should.
  4. \(h\propto N^{-1/5}\). With \(32\times\) more data, \(h\) halves. More data supports a sharper estimate, but slowly.

Where the \(\frac15\) comes from (sketch)

At a point \(x\), the KDE's squared bias grows like \(h^4\) (smoothing flattens peaks) and its variance shrinks like \(\frac1{Nh}\) (each estimate averages about \(Nh\) nearby points). Integrated over \(x\):

\[ \mathrm{AMISE}(h)=A\,h^4+\frac{B}{Nh},\qquad \frac{d}{dh}=4Ah^3-\frac{B}{Nh^2}=0\;\Rightarrow\;h^5=\frac{B}{4AN} \]

So \(h\propto N^{-1/5}\). Plugging in \(A\) and \(B\) for a Gaussian kernel and Gaussian truth gives the constant \(\sigma(4/3)^{1/5}\).

Robust spread: the MAD

\[ \mathrm{MAD}=\mathrm{median}\big(|x-\mathrm{median}(x)|\big),\qquad\hat\sigma=1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD} \]
  1. For \(x\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), \(|x-\mu|\) is below its median exactly half the time: \(P(|x-\mu|\le m)=0.5\). That means \(m/\sigma=\Phi^{-1}(0.75)\approx0.6745\).
  2. So \(\mathrm{MAD}\approx0.6745\,\sigma\), and \(\sigma\approx\mathrm{MAD}/0.6745=1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}\). The constant makes the MAD agree with \(\sigma\) on Gaussian data.
  3. Worked example. Data \(\{1,2,3,4,100\}\). Median 3; absolute deviations \(\{2,1,0,1,97\}\); their median is 1, so \(\hat\sigma=1.48\). The sample standard deviation is about 43.6, all because of one point.

More than one dimension

\[ h=\Big(\prod_{d=1}^{D}h_d\Big)^{1/D} \]

Example: \(h_1=0.2\), \(h_2=0.8\) gives \(h=\sqrt{0.16}=0.4\). A geometric mean is right for scale-like quantities: the "volume" \(h^D\) of the window equals \(\prod_dh_d\).

§16.3.4

From KDE to KNN: grow a balloon until it holds K points

§16.1 introduced the K nearest neighbor classifier as a heuristic. Here it falls out of a generative classifier: model each class-conditional density \(p(\mathbf x\mid y=c)\) with a KDE, use the class frequencies as the prior, and apply Bayes' rule.

The twist is the window. Instead of a fixed bandwidth, grow a region around \(\mathbf x\) until it contains \(K\) points of any class. This is a balloon kernel density estimator. Its volume \(V(\mathbf x)\) changes from query to query, and in Bayes' rule everything except the neighbor counts cancels.

Explain itAt your level

Blow up a balloon around the mystery point until it touches 10 marbles.

Some marbles are red and some are blue. When the balloon holds 10, count: 7 red and 3 blue. So you guess red, and you're 7-out-of-10 sure.

Where marbles are packed tight, the balloon stays small. Where they're spread out, it gets big. Either way, you always count exactly 10.

Grown-ups can use a lot of fancy math here, about how crowded each color is and how common each color is. But when they do all of it, the answer comes out to exactly the same thing: 7 out of 10.

To guess a label, ask two questions for each class: "How crowded is this class near \(\mathbf x\)?" (the density) and "How common is this class overall?" (the prior). Multiply, then compare.

Crowdedness = (how many class-\(c\) points are in the balloon) ÷ (all class-\(c\) points × balloon size). Commonness = (class-\(c\) points) ÷ (all points).

Multiply those and the "all class-\(c\) points" cancels. The balloon size is the same for every class, so it cancels too when you compare. What's left: the fraction of the \(K\) balloon points that are class \(c\). That's exactly the KNN vote from §16.1.

Let \(V(\mathbf x)\) be the volume of the smallest ball around \(\mathbf x\) holding \(K\) points, and \(N_c(\mathbf x)\) the number of those from class \(c\). The balloon KDE for class \(c\) (Eq. 16.34) is \(\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y=c)=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_cV(\mathbf x)}\): a count divided by (class size × volume), just like the boxcar KDE.

With prior \(p(y=c)=N_c/N\), Bayes' rule gives \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)\propto\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_cV}\cdot\frac{N_c}{N}=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{NV}\). The factor \(\frac1{NV}\) is the same for every class, so after normalizing, \(p(y=c\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{\sum_{c'}N_{c'}(\mathbf x)}=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}K\) (Eq. 16.35), which is Eq. 16.1.

The class sizes \(N_c\) cancel only because the prior is the empirical frequency. Use a different prior (say, uniform) and the KNN vote gets reweighted by \(1/N_c\).

  • Balloon vs sample-point. A balloon estimator (Terrell–Scott [TS92]) varies the bandwidth with the query \(\mathbf x\). The resulting \(\hat p(\mathbf x)=\frac{K}{NV_K(\mathbf x)}\) is the classical KNN density estimate; it is not a proper density (it doesn't integrate to 1; in 1-D its tails decay like \(1/|x|\), so the integral diverges), but its ratios are still fine for classification.
  • Fixed-window comparison. With a fixed boxcar window you get the same formula with \(\sum_cN_c(\mathbf x)\) in place of \(K\), but the total count can be 0 in sparse regions (posterior 0/0). The balloon guarantees \(K\) votes everywhere.
  • Consistency. The KNN density estimate is consistent if \(K\to\infty\) and \(K/N\to0\); so is the induced classifier (Stone, 1977), matching the conditions in §16.1.
  • Class priors under shift. If deployment priors \(\pi_c\) differ from training frequencies, the generative derivation tells you the fix: weight votes by \(\pi_cN/N_c\).

Math, slowlyEquations 16.34 – 16.35

The class-conditional density

\[ p(\mathbf x\mid y=c,\mathcal D)=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_c\,V(\mathbf x)} \tag{16.34} \]
  1. Start from the boxcar KDE for class \(c\) only: \(\frac{1}{N_c}\sum_{n:y_n=c}\frac{1}{V}\mathbb I(\mathbf x_n\in\text{window})\). In \(D\) dimensions the boxcar has height \(1/V\) so that it integrates to 1; in 1-D, \(V=2h\).
  2. The sum counts class-\(c\) points in the window: \(N_c(\mathbf x)\). So the density is \(\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_cV}\).
  3. Balloon twist: the window is the smallest ball around \(\mathbf x\) that holds \(K\) points of any class. Its volume \(V(\mathbf x)\) depends on \(\mathbf x\), and \(\sum_cN_c(\mathbf x)=K\).

Bayes' rule, term by term

\[ p(y=c\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)=\frac{\dfrac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_cV(\mathbf x)}\cdot\dfrac{N_c}{N}}{\sum_{c'}\dfrac{N_{c'}(\mathbf x)}{N_{c'}V(\mathbf x)}\cdot\dfrac{N_{c'}}{N}} =\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{\sum_{c'}N_{c'}(\mathbf x)}=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{K}=\frac1K\sum_{n\in N_K(\mathbf x,\mathcal D)}\mathbb I(y_n=c) \tag{16.35} \]
  1. Numerator: \(\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{N_cV}\cdot\frac{N_c}{N}=\frac{N_c(\mathbf x)}{NV}\). The class size \(N_c\) cancels against the prior.
  2. Every term of the denominator has the same \(\frac{1}{NV}\). Factor it out of top and bottom.
  3. \(\sum_{c'}N_{c'}(\mathbf x)=K\). The balloon was grown until it held exactly \(K\) points, whatever their labels.
  4. \(N_c(\mathbf x)/K\) is the fraction of the \(K\) nearest neighbors with label \(c\). That's Eq. 16.1, the KNN classifier.

Worked example. \(N=85\), class sizes \(45,25,15\). The balloon with \(K=10\) has radius 0.1, so \(V=\pi(0.1)^2=0.0314\), and holds \(6,3,1\) points. For class 0: \(p(\mathbf x\mid0)=\frac{6}{45\times0.0314}=4.24\), prior \(\frac{45}{85}=0.529\), product \(2.25\). Classes 1 and 2: \(\frac{3}{25\times0.0314}\cdot\frac{25}{85}=1.12\) and \(\frac{1}{15\times0.0314}\cdot\frac{15}{85}=0.375\). Normalize: \(\frac{2.25}{3.75}=0.6=\frac6{10}\).

The cancellation needs two things: the prior is the empirical frequency \(N_c/N\), and every class uses the same window. Change either and you get a weighted KNN instead.

§16.3.5.1

Nadaraya-Watson: predict a weighted average of nearby y's

For regression we want the conditional mean \(\mathbb E[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]\). Model the joint density \(p(\mathbf x,y)\) with a KDE that puts a product kernel on each pair \((\mathbf x_n,y_n)\). Integrate out \(y\), and the kernel facts from §16.3.1 collapse the result to a simple formula: a weighted average of the training outputs, with weights from the kernel in \(\mathbf x\).

This is kernel regression, also called kernel smoothing or the Nadaraya-Watson (N-W) model.

Explain itAt your level

How warm will it be at your friend's house?

You know the temperature at lots of houses around town. To guess for your friend's house, you listen most to houses right next door, a little to houses a few streets away, and not at all to houses across town.

Then you take a "listening-weighted" average. If the next-door houses say 70° and a far house says 50°, your guess will be close to 70°.

How far you're willing to listen is the bandwidth again. Listen to only the very closest house, and your guess jumps around. Listen to the whole town, and every house gets the same boring guess.

You have points \((x_n,y_n)\) on a scatter plot. To predict \(y\) at a new \(x\):

  1. Give each training point a weight from the kernel: big if \(x_n\) is close to \(x\), small if far.
  2. Divide every weight by the total so the weights add to 1.
  3. Prediction = \(\sum_n(\text{weight}_n)\times y_n\), a weighted average.

Slide \(x\) along and repeat, and you trace out a smooth curve through the cloud of points. Small \(h\): the curve chases every point. Large \(h\): it flattens toward the overall average of \(y\).

With the joint KDE \(p(\mathbf x,y\mid\mathcal D)\approx\frac1N\sum_nK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)K_h(y-y_n)\) (Eq. 16.37), the conditional mean is a ratio of integrals over \(y\). Two facts finish it: \(\int yK_h(y-y_n)\,dy=y_n\) (Eq. 16.25) and \(\int K_h(y-y_n)\,dy=1\).

Result (Eqs. 16.39–16.40): \(\mu(\mathbf x)=\sum_nw_n(\mathbf x)\,y_n\) with \(w_n(\mathbf x)=K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)/\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})\). The weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so \(\mu(\mathbf x)\) always lies between the smallest and largest \(y_n\).

The \(y\)-kernel drops out completely; only the \(\mathbf x\)-bandwidth matters for the mean. If you used a single Gaussian for \(p(\mathbf x,y)\) instead, you'd get linear regression (§11.2.3.5). The KDE removes that linearity restriction.

  • A linear smoother. \(\hat{\mathbf y}=S\mathbf y\) with \(S_{mn}=w_n(\mathbf x_m)\), rows summing to 1. Effective degrees of freedom \(=\mathrm{tr}(S)\); leave-one-out CV has the closed form \(\frac1N\sum_m\big(\frac{y_m-\hat y_m}{1-S_{mm}}\big)^2\), so choosing \(h\) is cheap.
  • Local constant fit. \(\mu(\mathbf x)=\arg\min_{\beta_0}\sum_n(y_n-\beta_0)^2K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\). §16.3.5.3 upgrades to a local line, which fixes N-W's bias at boundaries and where the design density \(p(\mathbf x)\) changes.
  • Bias. For interior points, \(\mathrm{bias}\approx h^2\mu_2(K)\big(\tfrac12m''(x)+\frac{m'(x)p'(x)}{p(x)}\big)\). The second term (design bias) is what local-linear removes.
  • Relation to GPs. The GP posterior mean is also linear in \(\mathbf y\), \(\mathbf k_*^\top(K+\sigma^2I)^{-1}\mathbf y\), but its "equivalent kernel" comes from a matrix inverse and can be negative. N-W weights are the kernel itself, normalized (§17.2.3).
  • Attention. Softmax attention is N-W with a learned similarity: \(w_n\propto\exp(\mathbf q^\top\mathbf k_n/\sqrt d)\), output \(\sum_nw_n\mathbf v_n\). A Gaussian kernel on \(\lVert\mathbf q-\mathbf k_n\rVert\) with equal-norm keys gives exactly this form.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.36 – 16.40

The goal: a conditional mean

\[ \mathbb E[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]=\int y\,p(y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D)\,dy=\frac{\int y\,p(\mathbf x,y\mid\mathcal D)\,dy}{\int p(\mathbf x,y\mid\mathcal D)\,dy} \tag{16.36} \]

The second form uses \(p(y\mid\mathbf x)=p(\mathbf x,y)/p(\mathbf x)\) and \(p(\mathbf x)=\int p(\mathbf x,y)\,dy\). So all we need is a model of the joint density.

A KDE for the joint

\[ p(y,\mathbf x\mid\mathcal D)\approx\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^{N}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\,K_h(y-y_n) \tag{16.37} \]

One product kernel per training pair. Plug it into both integrals of 16.36 and move the \(y\)-free factor \(K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\) outside each integral:

\[ \mathbb E[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]=\frac{\frac1N\sum_{n}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\,\class{tm}{\int y\,K_h(y-y_n)\,dy}}{\frac1N\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})\,\class{tg}{\int K_h(y-y_{n'})\,dy}} \tag{16.38} \]
  1. \(\int yK_h(y-y_n)\,dy=y_n\). Eq. 16.25: a symmetric kernel centered at \(y_n\) has mean \(y_n\).
  2. \(\int K_h(y-y_{n'})\,dy=1\). Density kernels integrate to 1.
  3. The \(\frac1N\) cancels between top and bottom. What's left:
\[ \mathbb E[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]=\frac{\sum_{n}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\,y_n}{\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}y_n\,w_n(\mathbf x) \tag{16.39} \]
\[ w_n(\mathbf x)\triangleq\frac{K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)}{\sum_{n'=1}^{N}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})} \tag{16.40} \]

Worked example. Three points with kernel values \(K_h(x-x_n)=2,1,1\) and outputs \(y_n=3,0,6\). Weights: \(\frac24,\frac14,\frac14\). Prediction: \(0.5\cdot3+0.25\cdot0+0.25\cdot6=3\).

With a compact kernel and small \(h\), a query with no training points within \(h\) has denominator 0, and \(\mu(\mathbf x)\) is undefined. Try the boxcar with \(h=0.02\) in the Play box: the curve breaks into pieces.

§16.3.5.2

The predictive variance: how spread out are the nearby y's?

The same joint KDE gives more than a mean. Use \(\mathbb V[y\mid\mathbf x]=\mathbb E[y^2\mid\mathbf x]-\mu(\mathbf x)^2\), and with a Gaussian \(y\)-kernel of variance \(\sigma^2\) the answer is \(\sigma^2\) plus the weighted variance of the neighbors' outputs (Eq. 16.45).

Explain itAt your level

Do the nearby houses agree?

If all the houses next to your friend's say 70°, you can be pretty sure it's about 70° there. If one says 60° and another says 80°, your best guess is still 70°, but you should say "somewhere between 60 and 80."

So besides a guess, we can give a "how sure am I" range. It's wide where the neighbors disagree and narrow where they agree.

Variance measures spread. The formula "average of the squares minus the square of the average" works for any list of numbers. Here we use the weighted average, with the same kernel weights as the prediction.

Example: two neighbors with equal weight and \(y=0\) and \(y=2\). Average = 1, average of squares = 2, so the spread is \(2-1^2=1\).

The book adds one more piece, \(\sigma^2\), the width of the little bump placed on each \(y_n\). It's the same everywhere, so it just widens the band evenly.

Plot the prediction ± 2 standard deviations, and you get a band that's wide where the data are noisy and narrow where they're tight.

\(\mathbb E[y^2\mid\mathbf x]\) uses the same ratio as 16.38, but with \(\int y^2\mathcal N(y\mid y_n,\sigma^2)\,dy=\sigma^2+y_n^2\) in the numerator (second moment = variance + mean²). That gives \(\sum_nw_n(\sigma^2+y_n^2)=\sigma^2+\sum_nw_ny_n^2\).

So \(\mathbb V[y\mid\mathbf x]=\sigma^2+\sum_nw_ny_n^2-\mu^2\). The last two terms are \(\sum_nw_n(y_n-\mu)^2\ge0\): the weighted variance of the outputs. This matches Eq. 8 of [BA10] up to the \(\sigma^2\) term.

This is a heteroscedastic noise estimate: the band changes width with \(\mathbf x\). Try the "noise grows with x" data.

  • What it measures. \(\mathbb V[y\mid\mathbf x]\) is the predictive variance of a new \(y\) (aleatoric noise plus local curvature smeared in by the window). It is not the variance of \(\hat\mu(\mathbf x)\) as an estimator, which is about \(\sum_nw_n^2\sigma^2(\mathbf x_n)\), i.e. noise ÷ effective sample size.
  • Bias inflation. Where \(m(\mathbf x)\) curves inside the window, \(\sum w_n(y_n-\mu)^2\) picks up \(m\)'s variation as if it were noise. Large \(h\) overstates the variance near peaks.
  • The \(\sigma^2\) term. With a non-Gaussian \(y\)-kernel, replace \(\sigma^2\) by \(h_y^2\mu_2(K)\). As \(h_y\to0\) it vanishes, which is why [BA10] omits it.
  • Practice. Common alternatives: smooth the squared residuals \((y_n-\hat\mu(\mathbf x_n))^2\) with a second kernel smoother, or use local likelihood / quantile regression for asymmetric noise.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.41 – 16.45

\[ \mathbb V[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]=\mathbb E\big[y^2\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D\big]-\mu(\mathbf x)^2 \tag{16.41} \]

This is the usual "mean of the square minus square of the mean," with \(\mu(\mathbf x)\) the N-W estimate.

The second moment

\[ \mathbb E\big[y^2\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D\big]=\frac{\sum_{n}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\int y^2K_h(y-y_n)\,dy}{\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})\int K_h(y-y_{n'})\,dy} \tag{16.42} \]
  1. Same derivation as 16.38, with \(y^2\) in place of \(y\). The denominator integral is still 1.
  2. With a Gaussian \(y\)-kernel of variance \(\sigma^2\): \[ \int y^2\,\mathcal N(y\mid y_n,\sigma^2)\,dy=\sigma^2+y_n^2 \tag{16.44} \] For any random variable, \(\mathbb E[y^2]=\mathbb V[y]+(\mathbb E[y])^2\).
  3. Substitute: \[ \mathbb E\big[y^2\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D\big]=\frac{\sum_nK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)(\sigma^2+y_n^2)}{\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})}=\sigma^2+\sum_nw_n(\mathbf x)\,y_n^2 \tag{16.43} \] The weights sum to 1, so the \(\sigma^2\) comes out on its own.

The variance

\[ \mathbb V[y\mid\mathbf x,\mathcal D]=\sigma^2+\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_n(\mathbf x)\,y_n^2-\mu(\mathbf x)^2 \tag{16.45} \]

Because \(\sum_nw_n=1\) and \(\mu=\sum_nw_ny_n\), the last two terms equal \(\sum_nw_n(y_n-\mu)^2\), so they are never negative:

\[ \sum_nw_n(y_n-\mu)^2=\sum_nw_ny_n^2-2\mu\sum_nw_ny_n+\mu^2\sum_nw_n=\sum_nw_ny_n^2-2\mu^2+\mu^2 \]

Worked example. \(\sigma^2=0.1\), two neighbors with \(w=0.5\) each and \(y=0,2\). Then \(\mu=1\), \(\sum w_ny_n^2=2\), and \(\mathbb V=0.1+2-1=1.1\).

§16.3.5.3

Locally weighted regression: fit a small line at every x

Nadaraya-Watson predicts a weighted average of nearby outputs, a local constant. If the data have a slope, an average is pulled toward whichever side has more data, which is a big problem at the edges. Locally linear regression (LOWESS or LOESS) fits a weighted least-squares line around each query and reports its value at the query.

The book first notes the unnormalized version, Eq. 16.46, which just drops the denominator from N-W. The Play box shows why that denominator matters.

Explain itAt your level

Guessing how tall a hill is at the very edge of the map.

You only have measurements to your left, because the map ends on your right. If you just average them, you get the height of the land behind you, which is lower than where you're standing on the slope.

A smarter trick: look at the nearby measurements, notice "it's going uphill," and follow that slope to your spot. That's what locally weighted regression does: draw a short straight line through the nearby points and read off its height where you are.

N-W regression is "average the nearby \(y\)'s." LOWESS is "fit a best-fit line to the nearby points, counting close points more, and use the line's value at \(x\)."

In the middle of the data, the nearby points are balanced on both sides, so the two methods mostly agree. At the edges, they're all on one side. The average then lags behind the trend, while the line keeps going in the right direction.

You see LOWESS all the time as the smooth trend line drawn on top of scatter plots.

At query \(\mathbf x\), solve the weighted least-squares problem \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}(\mathbf x)=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\sum_n[y_n-\boldsymbol\beta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_n)]^2K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\) with \(\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[1,x]\), then predict \(\mu(\mathbf x)=\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}(\mathbf x)^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\). (Eq. 16.47 writes this compactly as a min; the prediction is the fitted line evaluated at \(\mathbf x\).)

With \(\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[1]\), the same problem gives \(\beta_0=\sum_nw_ny_n\): that's N-W. So N-W is local-constant regression and LOWESS is local-linear.

Eq. 16.46, \(\sum_ny_nK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)\), skips dividing by \(\sum_nK_h\). Since \(\sum_nK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)=N\hat p(\mathbf x)\), it equals \(N\hat p(\mathbf x)\,\mu(\mathbf x)\): the regression curve multiplied by the data density. It sags toward 0 wherever data are sparse.

  • Closed form. \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}(\mathbf x)=(\Phi^\top W_{\mathbf x}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^\top W_{\mathbf x}\mathbf y\), \(W_{\mathbf x}=\mathrm{diag}(K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n))\). Still a linear smoother in \(\mathbf y\), but the equivalent weights can be negative, which is how it extrapolates the trend.
  • Bias. Local-linear bias \(\approx\frac{h^2}2\mu_2(K)m''(x)\), with no design-bias term and the same order at boundaries as in the interior (Fan, 1992). It is minimax-efficient among linear smoothers. Local polynomials of odd degree are generally preferred.
  • Cleveland's LOWESS [CD88] uses the tri-cube kernel with a nearest-neighbor bandwidth (the span: fraction of points in each window), plus a few robustness iterations that downweight points with large residuals via bisquare weights.
  • Cost. Naively one \(O(ND^2+D^3)\) solve per query; implementations evaluate on a grid of vertices and interpolate.

Math, slowlyEquations 16.46 – 16.47

Dropping the normalization

\[ \mu(\mathbf x)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}y_n\,K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n) \tag{16.46} \]

Compare with N-W: \(\sum_ny_nK_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n)=\Big(\sum_{n'}K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_{n'})\Big)\cdot\mu_{\text{NW}}(\mathbf x)=N\,\hat p(\mathbf x)\,\mu_{\text{NW}}(\mathbf x)\), where \(\hat p\) is the KDE of the inputs. So 16.46 is a weighted sum of responses whose overall size depends on how much data is nearby. The checkbox plots it divided by \(N\), i.e. \(\hat p(x)\,\mu_{\text{NW}}(x)\).

A line at every query

\[ \mu(\mathbf x)=\min_{\boldsymbol\beta}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big[y_n-\boldsymbol\beta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x_n)\big]^2K_h(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_n),\qquad\boldsymbol\phi(x)=[1,x] \tag{16.47} \]

Read this as: find the \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}(\mathbf x)\) that minimizes the weighted squared error, then predict \(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x\). In 1-D, write \(k_n=K_h(x-x_n)\) and the weighted sums

\[ S_0=\sum k_n,\quad S_1=\sum k_nx_n,\quad S_2=\sum k_nx_n^2,\quad T_0=\sum k_ny_n,\quad T_1=\sum k_nx_ny_n \]
  1. Set the derivatives with respect to \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) to 0. The normal equations of weighted least squares: \[ \begin{pmatrix}S_0&S_1\\S_1&S_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\\beta_1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}T_0\\T_1\end{pmatrix} \]
  2. Solve the 2×2 system: \(\hat\beta_1=\dfrac{S_0T_1-S_1T_0}{S_0S_2-S_1^2}\), \(\hat\beta_0=\dfrac{T_0-\hat\beta_1S_1}{S_0}\). \(\hat\beta_1\) is the weighted covariance of \(x\) and \(y\) over the weighted variance of \(x\).
  3. Predict \(\mu(x)=\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1x\). The Play box does exactly this at every pixel.
  4. Drop the slope (\(\boldsymbol\phi=[1]\)): the single equation \(S_0\beta_0=T_0\) gives \(\beta_0=T_0/S_0=\sum_nw_ny_n\). That's Nadaraya-Watson, Eq. 16.39.

Why the line wins at the edges

At the right edge every neighbor has \(x_nboundary bias, and it is of order \(h\). The local line uses the slope among the neighbors to extrapolate the last bit, so its bias is of order \(h^2\), the same as in the interior.
